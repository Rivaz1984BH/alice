Australia's Symbol

The kangaroo is more than an animal — it is the living emblem of a continent, appearing on the national coat of arms, coins, and the tail of Qantas jets.

The Kangaroo

Kangaroos are marsupials from the family Macropodidae — the "big-footed" mammals. Around 60 species live in Australia and New Guinea, from the towering red kangaroo of the outback to the tiny musky rat-kangaroo of the rainforest.

Why the Kangaroo Is Australia's Symbol

The kangaroo appears on the Australian coat of arms, granted in 1908, alongside the emu. Both animals are famous for moving forward rather than backward — a fitting symbol of national progress.

Kangaroos also appear on Australian coins, stamps, sports logos, and the tail of Qantas aircraft. The Boxing Kangaroo became a beloved sporting emblem.

For Aboriginal peoples, the kangaroo is a central figure in Dreaming stories, art, and traditional hunting — a relationship with the land stretching back tens of thousands of years.

Built for the Outback

Kangaroos are perfectly adapted to Australia's dry interior. Powerful hind legs and a thick, muscular tail form a three-part "tripod" for hopping, while the tail acts as a counterbalance at speed.

Their large ears radiate heat, and they can survive long periods with little water, obtaining moisture from plants. Females carry their young — called joeys — in a pouch for months after birth.

A red kangaroo can clear nine meters in a single bound and reach speeds of up to 70 km/h, making it one of the fastest large mammals on Earth.

Meet the Kangaroo Family

Red Kangaroo

It is the largest living marsupial, standing up to 2 m tall and roaming the arid interior.

Eastern Gray

It is common across eastern Australia and is often seen in grasslands, forests, and suburbs.

Tree Kangaroo

These agile climbers live in the rainforests of Queensland and New Guinea.

Wallaby

Wallabies are the kangaroo's smaller cousins, with around 30 species across Australia.

Life in the Pouch

A newborn joey is only about 2 cm long — roughly the size of a jellybean. It crawls into the mother's pouch, attaches to a teat, and grows there for months before exploring the world.

Social Mobs

Kangaroos live in groups called mobs, often led by a dominant male. They communicate with thumps, clicks, and gentle grooming, and they box to settle disputes.

Conservation

Most kangaroo species are secure, but tree kangaroos and several smaller macropods are threatened by habitat loss, predators, and climate change.

Did you know? The kangaroo and emu on Australia's coat of arms were chosen partly because neither animal can easily walk backward — symbolizing a nation always moving forward.
